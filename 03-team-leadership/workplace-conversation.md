# Module 3: Workplace conversation practice

Status: Drafted on 2026-09-21. Hypothetical Meridian scenario selected by Josh for the exercise. These are practice materials, not records of actual employee behavior. The role-play and debrief have not yet occurred.

## Step 1: Your situation

A product manager reporting to me has brought an executive-dashboard request back into each of our last three weekly sprint-planning meetings, despite our agreed priority of improving field adoption. They believe the dashboard should take priority because two customer renewals are at risk. These discussions have reopened prioritization decisions and reduced the time available to plan field-capture improvements. I communicated the roadmap priorities, but I did not establish how new commercial risks should trigger reconsideration or who makes that decision.

## Step 2: Diagnosis and reasoning

Clarity. Our priorities were explicit, but the process and decision rights for challenging them were not. My frustration is partly with repeated planning disruption, but I also contributed by treating the dashboard as a hard no without defining how we would evaluate evidence that could justify an exception. I need to understand the renewal risk before interpreting the product manager's persistence as a feedback or performance issue.

## Step 3: Next action for the next two weeks

This week, I will meet privately with the product manager to understand the renewal evidence and acknowledge the missing escalation process. Together with the account owner and engineering lead, we will assess what the customers actually need, possible alternatives, and the impact on our field-adoption commitments.

By the end of the first week, I will make and document a prioritization decision, including the rationale and conditions for revisiting it. We will agree that new evidence goes through a short trade-off review before sprint planning, with me accountable for the final priority decision. During the second week, I will check whether this process allows concerns to be heard while keeping planning focused.

### Opening using Situation → Behavior → Impact

“In our last three sprint-planning meetings, you brought the executive dashboard back for prioritization after we had agreed to focus on field adoption, which reopened the scope discussion and left less time to plan the field-capture work.”

### Follow-up to invite their perspective

“I realize I haven't made it clear how we should revisit priorities when a renewal is at risk. Can you walk me through what you've heard from those two customers and what you believe will happen if we defer the dashboard?”

I want to practice a workplace conversation before I have it for real. Treat this as role-play using the hypothetical situation and diagnosis in Steps 1 and 2 above.

Play the product manager reporting to me, not me. You are concerned about two customer renewals and worried that our field-adoption strategy leaves too little room for commercial evidence. Infer your feelings from the scenario, but treat all invented details as part of the hypothetical exercise.

- Respond realistically based on your concerns, not simply to agree with me.
- Do not resolve the tension in one move. If my opening makes you guarded or defensive, show that.
- Keep each reply to two or three sentences.
- Stay realistic but never abusive. If the situation involves safety, harassment, or a formal HR matter, say so and stop.
- Do not coach or break character until I say “step out.”
- I will open. After three exchanges I will say “step out.” Then identify where the conversation became tense and what I could try instead.
